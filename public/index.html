Authentication for humans

Why passkeys finally make sense — a visual companion to George Coldham’s talk.

Authentication gets stronger when we stop asking people to manage reusable secrets.

Follow the 20-slide story, revisit the key ideas, and keep the implementation references.

Keep the claim precise. Passkeys resist credential phishing and password reuse through public-key credentials scoped to a relying party. They do not eliminate account recovery risks, compromised devices, stolen sessions, or every form of social engineering.

01 · The human problem

Authentication asks too much of people

Password sign-in makes people repeatedly recognise fakes, choose the right secret, and judge prompts under pressure. These first slides explain why that burden is a design problem.

SLIDE 01

Authentication for humans

Passkeys change the work authentication asks of people. Instead of expecting everyone to remember reusable secrets, inspect every URL, and judge every prompt perfectly, the system can prove which service is being accessed.

Remember: Design the authentication system around people, not perfect people around the system.

SLIDE 02

Authentication keeps failing

Every sign-in asks someone to make security decisions while they are trying to do something else. When success depends on noticing every cue, every time, ordinary interruptions become part of the threat model.

Remember: Repeated human vigilance is a brittle security control.

SLIDE 03

The questions at the door

Password sign-in repeatedly asks: Is this the real site? Is this the right password? Is this code or approval part of the sign-in I started? Attackers build familiar-looking flows and urgency around those decisions.

Remember: Move as much security work as possible into the protocol.

SLIDE 04

A secret both sides know

A password is a shared secret: the user presents it and the service checks it against a protected verifier. A well-run service stores a salted password hash, not plaintext, but a convincing fake page can still trick a person into disclosing the password.

Remember: The weakness is the reusable secret users must present.

SLIDE 05

More layers, same secret

Password managers help people create and use unique passwords. MFA adds protection, and phishing-resistant methods are stronger than codes or push approvals. These controls still matter wherever passwords remain; passkeys change the underlying proof.

Remember: Keep useful safeguards, while reducing dependence on passwords.

02 · A different proof

Passkeys change what the service verifies

A passkey lets an authenticator sign a fresh challenge with a credential scoped to the service. Local unlock and remote proof are separate steps.

SLIDE 06

Proof, not a password

A passkey uses a public/private key pair. During sign-in, the service sends a fresh challenge. The authenticator signs it, and the service verifies the response with the registered public key. The private key is not sent to the website; a passkey provider may sync protected key material between a user’s devices.

Remember: The service receives proof of possession, not a reusable secret.

SLIDE 07

Local unlock, remote proof

A face, fingerprint, PIN, or other local method can unlock an authenticator. The website receives a WebAuthn response, not a face image or fingerprint. User verification may be requested by policy, and the service must validate the returned result.

Remember: The biometric unlocks the key; it is not the credential sent to the site.

03 · The security difference

Fake sites and reused passwords lose their leverage

Origin and RP ID scoping make a lookalike domain a mismatch, while distinct credentials remove the portable password that fuels credential stuffing.

SLIDE 08

A fake door can look real

The two login doors look alike. Their addresses differ: example.com and evil-example.com. A person in a hurry on a small screen can miss a subtle difference, even when they know what to look for.

Remember: Do not make URL inspection the only barrier against phishing.

SLIDE 09

The passkey does not match the fake site

A credential is scoped to its relying-party identifier (RP ID). The browser checks the requesting origin, and the service must validate the expected origin, RP ID, challenge, and response. A credential for example.com will not authenticate to evil-example.com. This resists credential phishing, but it does not prevent every attack on an account or device.

Remember: The system checks the site context instead of asking the user to spot every fake.

SLIDE 10

One reused password, many doors

Credential stuffing is the use of credentials obtained in one context against other services. Reusing a password makes that reuse possible. This is an understandable response to too many accounts, not a reason to blame the person.

Remember: Reusable passwords can connect otherwise separate accounts.

SLIDE 11

One passkey per service

Each relying party registers its own credential. The public key stored by a service cannot sign in to another service, and it is not a replayable password. A breach can still expose account data, sessions, or recovery channels.

Remember: A passkey for one service is not a skeleton key for every service.

SLIDE 12

Back to the task

Passkeys reduce some of the memory work, password reuse, and visual judgment that sign-in has demanded. People still need secure devices, clear enrolment, usable recovery, and protected sessions.

Remember: A better division of work can make sign-in both easier and safer.

04 · Building and operating it

Implementation choices still matter

Registration, sign-in, credential storage, provider choice, enrolment, and recovery all need deliberate policy and testing.

SLIDE 13

Registration: create and store a public key

The service creates a challenge and registration options. The browser invokes the authenticator, which creates a credential for the RP ID. The service validates the challenge, origin, RP ID, and response, then stores the credential ID and public key with the account.

Remember: Use a maintained WebAuthn library or identity platform for the ceremony.

SLIDE 14

Sign-in: unlock, sign, verify

For each sign-in, the service issues a fresh, unpredictable challenge. The authenticator signs the ceremony data, and the service verifies the signature, challenge, origin, RP ID, and any required user-verification flags before creating a session.

Remember: Fresh challenges and complete server-side verification matter.

SLIDE 15

SSH and WebAuthn share a primitive, not a protocol

SSH public-key authentication and WebAuthn both use public-key signatures. Their protocols define different trust contexts: SSH includes transport and host-key verification; WebAuthn uses the browser and authenticator and scopes a credential to a relying party and origin.

Remember: Shared cryptography does not make the protocols interchangeable.

SLIDE 16

Device-bound and synced passkeys

A device-bound passkey stays with one authenticator, which can fit tighter custody requirements but makes replacement and backup planning essential. A synced passkey improves availability across devices; provider account security and recovery become part of the trust model.

Remember: Choose based on assurance, recovery, and the people who must use it.

SLIDE 17

A phone can sign in to a laptop

Cross-device authentication lets a nearby phone complete a passkey sign-in that began on a laptop. The laptop can receive an authenticated session without receiving or storing the phone’s passkey.

Remember: A QR sign-in flow is not the same as syncing a passkey.

SLIDE 18

You own the policy

A platform or library handles the protocol details, but the application still owns decisions about RP ID, allowed origins, user verification, enrolment, account linking, and credential management. Treat changes to those settings as security and migration decisions.

Remember: Use mature implementation support, then configure and verify it carefully.

SLIDE 19

Make recovery usable before a key is lost

Plan what happens when a device is lost or replaced. Offer appropriate backup credentials, make credential revocation clear, and test the fallback path. A recovery route that is much weaker than sign-in can become the easiest way around it.

Remember: Recovery is part of authentication, not an afterthought.

05 · The takeaway

Fewer passwords

Passkeys do not solve every account risk. They remove some common password failure modes by design.

SLIDE 20

Not better password advice. Fewer passwords.

Passkeys are not magic and they do not end identity security work. They give us a better authentication primitive: service-scoped credentials and cryptographic proof instead of reusable secrets that people must carry between doors.

Remember: The human solution is to need fewer passwords.

The two WebAuthn ceremonies

Registration · create the credential

  1. The service creates a fresh challenge and RP options.
  2. The browser asks an authenticator to create a credential for the RP ID.
  3. The authenticator returns the credential ID and public key.
  4. The service validates the response and stores the public key.

Authentication · prove possession

  1. The service issues a fresh, unpredictable challenge.
  2. The authenticator signs the ceremony data, after any requested local verification.
  3. The service checks the signature, challenge, expected origin, RP ID, and policy flags.
  4. Only then does the service establish a session.

A practical checklist for teams

  • Use a maintained identity platform or WebAuthn server library; do not implement the cryptographic ceremony from scratch.
  • Set the RP ID and allowed origins deliberately. Validate the expected origin, RP ID, challenge, signature, and required flags.
  • Make enrolment, account linking, credential names, removal, and revocation understandable to users and support teams.
  • Plan for lost devices, provider recovery, backup credentials, migration, and fallback methods before rollout.
  • Test the full path on supported browsers and devices, including failure and recovery—not just a successful sign-in.

Quick definitions

A small glossary

Authenticator
A device, security key, or credential manager that protects and uses a passkey.
Credential
The relying-party-specific public-key credential used in a WebAuthn ceremony.
Origin
The web origin making the request, including its scheme, host, and port.
RP ID
The relying-party identifier that scopes where a WebAuthn credential can be used.
User verification
A local authenticator check, such as a PIN or biometric, when requested by the relying party.
Synced passkey
A passkey made available across a user’s devices through a passkey provider.
